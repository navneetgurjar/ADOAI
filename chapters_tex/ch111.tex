He had six men on the Great Hall roof and had been proud of it, in the quiet unexamined way a man is proud of a thing he has not yet troubled to ask himself honestly why he wants.

It was slow work. The eastern half had come down whole, and clearing it before anything could be rebuilt over it meant beams pried apart one at a time, timber sorted for salvage against timber fit only for the fire, and Jon had put himself into that work most mornings alongside the men doing it, partly because there was a satisfaction in it he had not expected and partly, though he had not examined this part closely, because there was something in seeing the old high table clear of rubble again that felt like an argument being won against something that had no interest in arguing back. The scarred hand caught on a length of rope his second morning at it, the old burn pulling tight and pale across the palm in a way that still surprised him more than it hurt, and after that he took to wrapping it before he climbed up, the way he’d have wrapped anything else that had already told him plainly what it would cost him to forget about it.

He did not notice the woman watching him from the yard until she had been standing there the better part of a quarter hour.

\sceneBreak

Her name was Tilda, and she had lost a husband to a fever that had gone through the outbuildings in the second month of the winter, in quarters that had never properly kept the weather out. Her daughter had lived through that fever once. Three nights before she finally climbed down to find him, the girl had gone under it a second time — not so badly as the first, Aldwin said when Jon asked him about it afterward, but badly enough that Tilda had sat up past the middle watch pressing wet cloth to a forehead too hot for a child that thin, in a room where the wind still came through the roof exactly as it had in autumn, because nobody had yet put a hand to mending it.

“You’ve six men up there,” she said, without preamble, when he finally came down for the midday meal. “I’ve counted them most days this fortnight, my lord, and I’d gone on being reasonable about it — told myself a man newly home to a burned house was owed some latitude in what he saw to first. I’ve run short of that reasonableness since three nights gone, when my girl’s fever came back on her and I sat up past the middle watch with cloths and no proper wall between us and the wind, on account of there’s eleven people sleeping in the north row under a roof with a hole in it you could put your fist through, and I keep being told the men are needed on the Hall.”

“The Hall matters.”

“Does it.” Tilda did not say it unkindly, which he found, afterward, made it considerably harder to dismiss than if she had. “It matters to you, my lord, and I’ll not pretend I don’t understand why — you grew up under that roof and it’s the one thing standing here that still looks like the place you lost, and I’d be a poor sort of person to tell you that doesn’t count for anything. But there’s eleven people in the north row who’ve had a hole in their roof since the first snow, and not one of them is going to be warmer this winter because the high table’s been dug out from under a beam.”

\sceneBreak

He heard himself begin the defense before he had properly decided to make it, and heard, somewhere in the middle of making it, exactly how thin it sounded once it was actually spoken aloud rather than simply held, comfortably, in his own head.

“There’s a use in it beyond warmth. A hall standing whole again — it tells people something. That the North isn’t only ruin, that there’s a place left that still looks like what it was.”

“Aye, and I’d agree with you, my lord, if the eleven people in the north row had a roof over them first and the Hall came after. I’d cheer the Hall myself, once my own girl’s stopped waking in the night from the cold coming through where the thatch has given way. My girl had a second night of it three days gone, my lord, while your six men were up there sorting timber. She’ll live. She lived the first time too. But you’ve had six men on that roof for a fortnight and none on mine, and I don’t think that’s a choice you made because it was the right one. I think it’s a choice you made because clearing that table feels like doing something for your father, and clearing my roof only feels like doing something for a widow you don’t know, and I’ll grant those are different feelings, my lord, but I’ll not grant they ought to decide where six men’s labor goes of a morning.”

\sceneBreak

Jon did not answer that immediately, because he found, turning it over, that he did not have an answer that survived being turned over honestly, and he had learned, this past year, at some cost, that an answer which only worked so long as it wasn’t examined too closely was not, in fact, an answer at all.

“I’ll put three of the six on your row this afternoon,” he said, at last, “and finish clearing the table with the other three, because it’s near enough done now that leaving it half-cleared serves nobody, yours or mine.”

It was not the answer she had come down for, and he could see her weighing whether to say so and deciding, in the end, that three was more than none.

“Three’s more than none, I suppose,” Tilda said. “I’ll not pretend it’s what I asked you for, my lord.” She turned to go, then stopped. “I didn’t come down here to be thanked for saying a true thing, if that’s where this is headed.”

“It wasn’t headed there,” Jon said, though he heard, saying it, that he was not entirely certain yet what he owed her instead.

\sceneBreak

He told himself three men was a fair compromise, and went on believing it for exactly as long as it took to walk past the north row on his way to the godswood that evening.

There was lantern light coming through the gap Tilda had described, a hole a fist could pass through, and it had not gotten any smaller for three men’s work between midday and dark, because three men can only do so much in an afternoon, and none of what they had managed yet had closed anything a fever cared about. He stood in the yard a while longer than he meant to, past where anyone would have seen him standing there, and thought of the high table, half a stride from fully clear, and found that the argument for finishing it, which had sounded entirely reasonable that morning, would not reassemble itself for him a second time.

He moved the remaining three men at first light, without being asked to a second time, and told himself it was not because Tilda had been right — she had been right well before he’d finished defending himself against it — but because a compromise that let a child cough through a second night while a table sat nearly done was not, looked at honestly in the cold of a morning rather than the comfort of an afternoon, a compromise at all.

“Well,” Tilda said, when she found all six of them on her row instead of three. “That took you longer than I’d have liked, my lord, and I’ll say so, but it’s more than most lords manage even slowly. I’ll take it.”

\sceneBreak

The Great Hall’s roof sat half-cleared and unfinished for the better part of a month afterward, open to the sky on its eastern side in a manner that Jon found, walking past it each morning, troubled him rather less than he had expected an unfinished thing to trouble him.

He wrote to Sansa that week, and told her about Tilda, and about the six men, and did not spare himself in the telling of how long the whole business had taken — three days, start to finish, from the morning Tilda first came down off the scaffold to the morning he finally moved the last three men, and no single one of those three days he could afterward call himself blameless in. He told her he thought he had wanted the Hall whole again for his father’s sake more than he’d have owned to at the time, which was true, and which he was fairly sure was not the whole of it, though he could not, writing it, put a name to the rest, and said so plainly in the letter rather than pretend to an answer his own head hadn’t finished arriving at.

\sceneBreak

The Great Hall’s roof was finished, eventually, months later, by men who had proper shelter to come home to by then, and Jon stood under it the day it was closed against the weather again and found that it felt like considerably less of a victory than he had once imagined it would, and considerably more like simply a roof, doing the ordinary unglamorous work a roof was meant to do, over a room that would take a great deal longer than any single winter to feel like the place he remembered.

He thought that was probably the more honest way to have it feel, in the end, and did not try to talk himself into feeling any more than that about it.
